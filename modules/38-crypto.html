Module 38: Crypto

Blockchain, Bitcoin, DeFi, and the promises and problems of decentralised finance

Part A - the problem crypto tries to solve - trust without intermediaries
Why this matters
Crypto is a coordination technology, not magic money
The core idea is stark: can strangers maintain one shared ledger without a bank, state, or platform owner deciding what is true? Bitcoin answered with proof-of-work. Ethereum added programmable contracts. DeFi, stablecoins, NFTs, DAOs, and the regulatory fight are all consequences of that first design move.
Roadmap
From keys to crashes

Mental model
A database with economic armor
A blockchain is not efficient in the ordinary software sense. It repeats work across many machines on purpose, because redundancy is the price of not trusting one operator. The question is always whether censorship resistance, open settlement, and self-custody are worth that cost in a given use case.
The common misconception is that Bitcoin made copying digital files impossible. It did not. It made it economically and socially difficult to rewrite a particular shared history of ownership. That is subtler, and much more powerful.

Proof-of-work
expensive truth

Miners repeatedly hash candidate blocks until one falls below the network target. The work is useless except for one purpose: it makes rewriting history expensive. Bitcoin adjusts difficulty every 2,016 blocks, roughly two weeks, so blocks keep arriving near a 10-minute average even when mining hardware changes.

Proof-of-stake
bonded truth

Validators lock capital and risk losing it if they violate consensus rules. Ethereum's September 15, 2022 Merge removed proof-of-work mining and cut Ethereum energy use by about 99.95 percent. The tradeoff is different: less energy, but more attention to stake concentration, governance, and client diversity.

Wallets
keys, not coins

A wallet usually holds private keys, not cryptocurrency itself. The coins or tokens are ledger entries; the private key authorizes movement. A 12 or 24 word seed phrase can recreate keys, which is elegant and terrifying: lose it and nobody can reset your account, leak it and nobody can reverse the theft.

Bitcoin's 21 million supply distribution by issuance era
The first four subsidy eras issued 93.75 percent of all bitcoin that will ever exist. The remaining 6.25 percent is stretched across more than a century, which is why Bitcoin's monetary story is mostly about early issuance and later scarcity.
Bitcoin is often called "digital gold," but the analogy is imperfect. Gold has industrial use, jewelry demand, and thousands of years of social history. Bitcoin has programmed scarcity, portability, auditability, and no issuer. The store-of-value thesis is not that Bitcoin is already stable; it is that a credible fixed-supply asset can become more stable if ownership and liquidity keep broadening.

Part E - DeFi - decentralised finance
Uniswap and AMMs
prices from pools

Uniswap's x*y=k design lets people trade against a pool instead of an order book. If a pool has 100 ETH and 300,000 USDC, the implied price is about 3,000 USDC per ETH before fees and slippage. Arbitrageurs keep the pool near external market prices, which means liquidity providers are effectively selling what rises and buying what falls.

Lending protocols
collateral first

Aave and Compound do not lend like banks. They usually overcollateralize loans, so a borrower might deposit 10,000 dollars of ETH to borrow 6,000 dollars of USDC. If collateral falls too far, liquidators repay debt and seize collateral at a discount. This is automated credit without personal credit scoring.

Stablecoins
crypto's dollar rail

Stablecoins are the workhorse of crypto because they remove volatility while keeping blockchain settlement. USDT and USDC are fiat-backed promises; DAI is crypto-collateralized; TerraUSD was algorithmic and collapsed in May 2022. The lesson is simple: a stablecoin is only as good as its collateral, redemption mechanism, and panic behavior.

The word "yield" is dangerous in DeFi. Sometimes it means real fees paid by traders or borrowers. Sometimes it means token emissions that dilute holders. Sometimes it means hidden leverage. The right question is never "what APY?" It is "who is paying me, why, in what asset, and what can break?"
Part F - NFTs, DAOs, and other crypto applications
Application reality scale: proven utility to speculative theatre

NFTs did not mean "you own the JPEG." Usually they meant you owned a token whose metadata points to media, rights, or a community promise. The strongest NFT use cases are not expensive profile pictures; they are scarce digital objects, tickets, game items, provenance records, membership credentials, and royalty-aware editions where the token is part of a larger system.
Part G - regulation, risks, and the bigger picture
What can fail in crypto systems
Percentages are a teaching weight, not an industry measurement. Real incidents often combine categories: FTX was custody plus governance plus fraud; Terra was design plus liquidity plus reflexive incentives.
Real innovations
settlement, self-custody, composability

Crypto proved that open networks can move value globally, keep public audit trails, and let software contracts compose like APIs. Stablecoin settlement, Bitcoin custody, and Ethereum smart contracts are not imaginary achievements. The useful parts are strongest where existing finance is slow, exclusionary, expensive, or politically risky.

Failed promises
mass adoption, fairness, easy yield

Many promises collapsed under incentives. Token voting often gives whales control. "Banking the unbanked" can become "selling volatile risk to people with fewer protections." Decentralisation can be superficial when front ends, bridges, stablecoins, venture allocations, and governance keys remain concentrated.

Sanity check
ask who bears loss

Every crypto design has a loss allocation rule. In a bank failure, deposit insurance, regulators, shareholders, creditors, and courts fight over loss. In crypto, loss may fall instantly on key holders, liquidity providers, token buyers, bridge users, or governance voters. The absence of an intermediary does not mean the absence of trust. It means trust moved.

Part H · Q&A

If blockchains are so inefficient, why use them at all?

Use them when the inefficiency buys something valuable: censorship resistance, shared settlement between parties that do not trust one operator, self-custody, or public auditability. A normal database is better for a payroll app, a private inventory system, or a social network run by one company. A blockchain starts making sense when the operator itself is the risk. That is why the strongest cases cluster around money, settlement, and assets that need neutral infrastructure.

Is Bitcoin anonymous?

Bitcoin is pseudonymous, not anonymous. Addresses are strings, but every transaction is public forever, so blockchain analytics firms can cluster behavior and connect addresses to exchanges, sanctions lists, hacks, and real identities. Privacy can improve with careful coin control or privacy tools, but ordinary users leak information constantly. Cash is often more private in daily life than transparent blockchains.

Did Ethereum's Merge make transactions cheaper?

No. The Merge changed Ethereum's consensus from proof-of-work to proof-of-stake, and it cut energy use by about 99.95 percent. It did not by itself increase execution capacity enough to make gas cheap. The cheaper-transaction strategy is rollups plus data availability improvements, such as the Dencun upgrade in 2024, not the Merge alone.

Are stablecoins safer than volatile crypto?

They are safer on price volatility, but they introduce issuer, reserve, legal, and redemption risk. A fiat-backed stablecoin depends on the quality and accessibility of its reserves. A crypto-backed stablecoin depends on collateral liquidation during stress. An algorithmic stablecoin that relies mainly on confidence can fail with brutal speed, as TerraUSD showed in May 2022.

Was FTX a failure of blockchain technology?

FTX was mostly a failure of centralized custody, governance, internal controls, and fraud. Customers thought they had assets held by an exchange; the business allegedly treated customer assets as available funding for related trading activity. That is not a smart contract exploit. The irony is sharp: one of crypto's biggest disasters looked a lot like an old-fashioned financial fraud with a crypto wrapper.

What is the best single question before buying any token?

Ask: what gives this token durable demand that is not just the hope someone later pays more? For Bitcoin, the answer is fixed-supply settlement and monetary premium. For ETH, it is payment for blockspace, staking, and collateral in an application ecosystem. For many tokens, the honest answer is weak: governance rights nobody uses, emissions, insider unlocks, or vibes with a ticker symbol.
