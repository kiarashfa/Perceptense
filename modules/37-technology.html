Module 37: Technology

How computers work, how the internet runs, and what AI actually is

Part A - what technology is - a brief history of tools and the digital revolution
Overview
Technology is organized leverage
A tool stores a trick so the next person can use it without rediscovering it. This module moves from that basic idea to chips, software, networks, data, AI, and frontier fields. The through-line is simple: technology converts scarce human attention into repeatable systems.
Parts A-B
From tools to hardware
We start with the long arc from stone tools to smartphones, then open the machine: binary, logic gates, CPUs, memory, storage, and the semiconductor supply chain that makes modern life fragile in very specific places.
Parts C-E
Software, networks, data
Software is layered abstraction, the web is a set of agreements, and databases are machines for preserving truth under pressure. The internet feels weightless, but it runs through fiber, power plants, contracts, and millions of fallible servers.
Parts F-G
AI and the frontier
Artificial intelligence is not magic or a mind in a box. It is statistical machinery trained at industrial scale. We close with quantum computing, biotechnology, robotics, and the habit that matters most: separating real capability from theatrical hype.
Technology waves, 10,000 BCE to today
Bars are deliberately approximate. A technology does not end when the next one begins; agriculture, steam engines, radio, and mainframes still matter.
A useful definition: technology is not "gadgets." It is any reliable method for amplifying human capability: a stone blade, double-entry bookkeeping, refrigeration, GPS, antibiotics, a search engine, or a shipping container. The quiet technologies often change the world more than the flashy ones.
Part B - how computers work - the hardware
Binary
0 / 1

Computers use binary because physical devices are good at distinguishing two stable states: high or low voltage, magnetized one way or the other, charged or uncharged. A bit is one such choice. Eight bits make a byte, enough to represent 256 values, which is why early character encodings and color channels lean so heavily on 8-bit boundaries.

Logic
Gates

Logic gates are tiny circuits that implement Boolean operations. Combine enough AND, OR, and NOT gates and you can build adders, memory cells, processors, and eventually a machine that plays video, routes satellites, or trains a neural network. The abstraction is astonishing: arithmetic becomes electrical choreography.

Binary converter
AND
Returns 1 only if both inputs are 1. It is the logic of permission: this condition and that condition must both be true.
OR
Returns 1 if either input is 1. It is the logic of alternatives: Wi-Fi or cellular, cache hit or database lookup.
NOT
Flips 1 to 0 and 0 to 1. Tiny inversions are enough to build memory, arithmetic, and control flow.
XOR
Returns 1 when inputs differ. It is central in binary addition, checksums, cryptography, and error detection.

CPU versus GPU

A CPU is optimized for low-latency, branching, general-purpose work: run the operating system, coordinate programs, handle irregular tasks. A GPU is optimized for throughput: thousands of simpler cores doing similar math at once. That is why GPUs became central to deep learning, where training a model means multiplying enormous matrices again and again.

Semiconductors

A cutting-edge chip can contain tens of billions of transistors. Manufacturing one requires extreme ultraviolet lithography, atom-scale deposition, ultrapure chemicals, and a supply chain spread across the Netherlands, Taiwan, Japan, Korea, the United States, and elsewhere. The "nanometer node" is now more brand label than literal transistor dimension, but it still tracks density, speed, and cost.

Variables and types

A variable names a value; a type says what operations make sense. A date, a string, a floating-point number, and a user record may all look like "data," but confusing them creates subtle bugs. The Mars Climate Orbiter was lost in 1999 after one software component used imperial units and another expected metric.

Loops and conditionals

Most programs are repetition plus decisions. Loop through customers, frames, pixels, packets, or transactions; branch when the price is too high, a password fails, or a sensor reads danger. The magic feeling of software comes from executing tiny, boring rules at inhuman speed.

Complexity

An algorithm that checks every pair in a million-item list performs roughly 500 billion comparisons. A better data structure can turn the same task into a few million operations. Big-O notation is not academic decoration; it is how a program crosses from instant to impossible.

What happens when you open a web page
Open source is not a hobby niche. Linux runs most servers, Android phones, cloud infrastructure, routers, and supercomputers. The strange economics are the point: companies share basic plumbing because competing on every line of code would be slower and more expensive than building together.
Part D - the internet and networks
Packets

The internet does not send your video call as one continuous object. It chops data into packets, each with addressing information, and routes them across many possible paths. TCP checks that packets arrive and asks for missing ones again; UDP skips some guarantees for speed, which is why it is common in games, voice, and video.

Physical infrastructure

Most international internet traffic travels through submarine fiber-optic cables, not satellites. Light pulses carry data through glass strands across ocean floors. A single modern cable pair can carry terabits per second, but landing stations, peering agreements, power, and politics decide where the network is fast, cheap, or vulnerable.

Part E - data and databases
Relational databases

SQL databases organize facts into tables with defined relationships. They are excellent when correctness matters: bank transfers, orders, inventory, medical records. ACID properties mean transactions should be atomic, consistent, isolated, and durable, which is a fancy way of saying "do not lose or half-save reality."

NoSQL databases

NoSQL is not one technology. It includes document stores, key-value stores, graph databases, and wide-column systems. They trade some rigid structure for scale, flexibility, or specialized queries. A social graph, a shopping cart cache, and a log pipeline want different shapes of storage.

Big data

"Big" starts when one machine and one tidy database stop being enough. Hadoop made distributed storage and batch processing mainstream in the 2000s; Spark made many jobs faster by keeping data in memory. The hard part is rarely storage. The hard part is deciding which data is trustworthy.

The phrase "data is the new oil" is catchy and wrong in one important way. Oil is used up when burned; data can be copied, combined, and reused indefinitely. That makes data less like fuel and more like memory with economic power attached.

A frontier technology can be scientifically real and economically immature at the same time. The winning question is not "is it possible?" but "what exact job does it do better than the existing system?"
The frontier is usually uneven. AlphaFold transformed protein-structure prediction years before robots could fold laundry reliably. Electric vehicles arrived before fully autonomous taxis. Technology does not advance as one smooth front; it breaks through wherever physics, data, money, regulation, and user tolerance happen to align.
Part H · Q&A
If computers are just 0s and 1s, why do they feel so flexible?
Because layers of abstraction compound. A transistor makes a bit reliable; logic gates combine bits; instructions combine gates into arithmetic and memory; programming languages combine instructions into human-readable commands. By the time you reach a browser or game engine, the primitive 0/1 choices are buried under millions of reusable conventions. Flexibility comes from stacking simple reliable layers until the top layer feels expressive.
Was Moore's Law a law of physics?
No. Gordon Moore observed in 1965 that the number of components on integrated circuits was rising at a striking pace, later simplified to transistor counts doubling roughly every two years. It held because physics, manufacturing, design tools, and enormous capital investment all reinforced each other. The old version has slowed because power density, quantum effects, and fabrication cost became brutal. Progress continues through chiplets, specialized accelerators, packaging, software, and parallelism.
Why is cybersecurity so hard if encryption is mathematically strong?
Encryption protects data under specific assumptions: keys stay secret, endpoints are honest, software is patched, and users are not tricked. Attackers often avoid the math entirely. They phish a password, exploit an old library, bribe an insider, steal a session token, or compromise a vendor. Security is a system property, and the system includes tired people, old code, business incentives, and emergency exceptions.
What does the cloud actually change?
The cloud converts computing from equipment you buy into capacity you rent. That matters because demand is spiky: a startup can scale from one server to thousands without building a data center. It also centralizes power in hyperscalers that can afford custom chips, global fiber, cooling expertise, and security teams. The tradeoff is dependence: outages, pricing changes, data residency rules, and vendor lock-in become strategic risks.
Do large language models understand anything?
It depends what you mean by understand. They do not have human experience, goals, or grounded perception by default, but they learn rich statistical structure from text, code, images, and feedback. That structure can support translation, summarization, programming, planning, and explanation in ways that look meaningfully cognitive. The practical stance is neither worship nor dismissal: treat them as powerful pattern engines that need verification, context, and good judgment around consequences.
How should I judge a new technology claim?
Ask what bottleneck it removes, what it costs, and what must change around it for adoption. A lab demo is not a supply chain, a prototype is not a business, and a benchmark is not a user habit. Look for constraints: energy, latency, regulation, manufacturing yield, training data, maintenance, liability, and integration with old systems. The future usually arrives through boring compatibility work after the spectacular demo.
